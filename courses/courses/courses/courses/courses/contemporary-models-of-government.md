## Course Overview

To understand the major democratic systems of the modern world, it is essential to examine political systems that have experimented with democratic principles over a long period of time.

This course studies four influential constitutional models:

- The United Kingdom’s parliamentary and evolutionary constitutional model.
- The United States’ presidential, federal, and separation-of-powers model.
- France’s semi-presidential and unitary model.
- Switzerland’s federal, direct-democratic, and collegial executive model.

These systems represent historically important patterns of organizing government. Their constitutional principles, institutional arrangements, and democratic practices have influenced political systems across the world, including constitutional systems in South Asia and elsewhere.

The course develops students’ ability to:

- Understand the basic organization of democratic constitutions.
- Classify modern democratic political systems.
- Apply the comparative method.
- Examine executive-legislative relations.
- Analyze territorial distribution of power.
- Evaluate constitutional checks and balances.
- Understand the relationship between institutions and democratic practice.
- Develop a foundation for studying the Indian Government comparatively.

The United Kingdom is studied through parliamentary sovereignty, constitutional conventions, responsible government, and the relationship between the Crown, Parliament, and executive. The United States is examined through presidential government, federalism, separation of powers, checks and balances, and judicial review. France provides a model of a semi-presidential and unitary system, while Switzerland demonstrates federalism, direct democracy, and a collegial executive.

In the United Kingdom, parliamentary sovereignty means that Parliament is the supreme legal authority and can create or end any law, although constitutional practice also involves conventions, judicial principles, devolution, and political accountability. [44][49]

The United States Constitution divides federal power among legislative, executive, and judicial branches and combines separation of powers with checks and balances. [45][46][48]

# Unit I: The United Kingdom

## 1. Salient Features of the Constitution

### Evolutionary Constitution

The British constitutional system developed gradually through historical changes rather than through the adoption of a single constitutional document at one moment.

Important sources include:

- Statutes.
- Judicial decisions.
- Constitutional conventions.
- Parliamentary practice.
- Historical documents.
- Political customs.
- Constitutional principles.
- International and domestic legal developments.

### Constitutional Conventions

Conventions are political rules and practices that guide the operation of constitutional institutions even when they may not be directly enforceable in court.

Examples include:

- The Prime Minister is generally the leader who can command the confidence of the House of Commons.
- The monarch usually acts on ministerial advice.
- The Cabinet is collectively responsible to Parliament.
- Ministers are accountable to Parliament.
- The government normally requires the confidence of the House of Commons.

### Parliamentary Sovereignty

Parliamentary sovereignty is a central principle of the UK constitutional system.

It generally means:

- Parliament is the supreme law-making authority.
- Parliament can create or repeal laws.
- Courts traditionally do not invalidate Acts of Parliament.
- One Parliament cannot permanently bind a future Parliament.
- Government is politically accountable to Parliament.

The UK Parliament explains parliamentary sovereignty as the principle that Parliament is the supreme legal authority and can create or end any law. [44][49]

### Rule of Law

The rule of law involves:

- Government according to law.
- Equality before the law.
- Legal accountability.
- Independent courts.
- Protection against arbitrary power.
- Fair and predictable legal procedures.
- Judicial protection of rights.

## 2. The King and the Prime Minister

### The King

The King is the constitutional head of state.

The monarch’s formal functions may include:

- Appointing the Prime Minister.
- Opening Parliament.
- Giving Royal Assent to legislation.
- Inviting the formation of a government.
- Performing ceremonial functions.
- Representing national continuity.
- Acting according to constitutional conventions.

### The Prime Minister

The Prime Minister is the head of government.

Major functions include:

- Leading the government.
- Selecting ministers.
- Coordinating Cabinet policy.
- Advising the monarch.
- Directing national policy.
- Representing the government internationally.
- Managing the relationship with Parliament.
- Exercising political leadership within the governing party or coalition.

## 3. Parliament

The UK Parliament consists of:

- The House of Commons.
- The House of Lords.
- The Crown as part of the constitutional law-making framework.

### House of Commons

The House of Commons is the elected chamber.

Major functions include:

- Making laws.
- Representing citizens.
- Controlling public finance.
- Scrutinizing the executive.
- Debating public issues.
- Approving taxation and expenditure.
- Determining which government can command confidence.
- Holding ministers politically accountable.

### House of Lords

The House of Lords is the second chamber.

Major functions include:

- Revising legislation.
- Scrutinizing government policy.
- Debating public issues.
- Providing specialist expertise.
- Reviewing bills.
- Performing a checking and delaying function.

The two Houses participate in legislation, government scrutiny, and public debate, while the Commons has a central role in determining the government’s political authority. [50][42]

### Comparative Significance

The UK model demonstrates:

- Parliamentary government.
- Responsible government.
- Fusion of executive and legislature.
- Constitutional flexibility.
- Political accountability.
- Importance of conventions.
- Parliamentary sovereignty.

# Unit II: The United States

## 1. Salient Features of the Constitution

### Presidential Executive

The United States has a presidential system in which the President serves as head of state and head of government.

Characteristics include:

- Fixed constitutional term.
- Executive authority vested in the President.
- Institutional separation from Congress.
- Cabinet appointed by the President.
- Political leadership independent of a parliamentary vote of confidence.

### Separation of Powers and Checks and Balances

The US Constitution divides federal power among:

- Legislative branch.
- Executive branch.
- Judicial branch.

The legislative branch makes laws, the executive branch implements laws, and the judiciary interprets law. Each branch has powers that may limit the others. [45][46][48]

Examples of checks and balances include:

- Presidential veto.
- Congressional law-making.
- Senate confirmation of appointments.
- Congressional control of public finance.
- Judicial review.
- Impeachment.
- Treaty and appointment procedures.
- Judicial protection of constitutional principles.

### Federal Structure

The United States is a federal system in which powers are divided between:

- Federal government.
- State governments.
- Local governments.

Federalism includes:

- Constitutional allocation of powers.
- State autonomy.
- Federal supremacy within constitutional limits.
- Intergovernmental relations.
- Judicial interpretation of federal boundaries.

### Judicial Review

Judicial review allows courts to examine whether governmental action is consistent with the Constitution.

It may involve:

- Review of legislation.
- Review of executive action.
- Protection of constitutional rights.
- Interpretation of constitutional provisions.
- Resolution of federal disputes.

## 2. The President

### Procedure of Election

The President is elected through a constitutionally established electoral process involving:

- Political parties.
- Primaries and conventions.
- General election.
- Electoral College.
- Formal constitutional procedures.

### Powers and Position

The President:

- Leads the executive branch.
- Serves as Commander-in-Chief.
- Conducts foreign policy.
- Appoints officials subject to constitutional procedures.
- Signs or vetoes legislation.
- Directs federal administration.
- Represents the United States internationally.
- Exercises constitutionally defined executive authority.

## 3. Congress

Congress is the federal legislature and consists of:

- Senate.
- House of Representatives.

### Senate

The Senate:

- Represents the states.
- Participates in legislation.
- Confirms certain presidential appointments.
- Provides advice and consent regarding treaties.
- Conducts oversight.
- Participates in impeachment procedures.

### House of Representatives

The House:

- Represents congressional districts.
- Initiates revenue legislation.
- Makes laws.
- Conducts oversight.
- Participates in impeachment proceedings.
- Debates national policy.

## 4. The Judiciary

### Composition

The federal judiciary includes:

- Supreme Court.
- Lower federal courts.
- Courts established under federal law.

### Jurisdiction and Functions

The judiciary:

- Interprets the Constitution.
- Interprets federal law.
- Resolves disputes.
- Reviews government action.
- Protects constitutional rights.
- Resolves federal and state legal questions.

### Comparative Significance

The US model demonstrates:

- Presidential government.
- Strong separation of powers.
- Checks and balances.
- Federalism.
- Constitutional supremacy.
- Judicial review.
- Fixed executive tenure.

# Unit III: France

## 1. Salient Features of the Constitution

### Mixed Parliamentary and Presidential System

France is commonly studied as a semi-presidential system combining:

- A directly elected or popularly legitimized President.
- A Prime Minister and Government.
- A Parliament.
- Shared or divided executive authority.
- Possibility of cohabitation when the President and parliamentary majority belong to different political camps.

### Unitary Form of Government

France is traditionally classified as a unitary state, although the country also contains decentralization and territorial administrative arrangements.

Characteristics include:

- National constitutional authority.
- Central government institutions.
- Uniform national legal framework.
- Administrative decentralization.
- Regional and local authorities operating within constitutional and statutory arrangements.

### Written and Rigid Constitution

The French constitutional system is based on a written constitutional document.

A relatively rigid constitution generally requires special procedures for formal amendment, distinguishing constitutional rules from ordinary legislation.

### Constitutional Council

The Constitutional Council performs constitutional review functions.

Its role may include:

- Reviewing legislation.
- Examining electoral disputes.
- Monitoring constitutional conformity.
- Reviewing referendum-related questions.
- Protecting constitutional principles.

### Referendum

Referendums provide a mechanism through which certain political or constitutional questions may be submitted directly to voters.

The referendum raises important questions concerning:

- Popular sovereignty.
- Executive leadership.
- Direct participation.
- Constitutional change.
- Majority rule.
- Political legitimacy.

## 2. The President

The President is the head of state.

Major areas of presidential authority may include:

- Appointment of the Prime Minister.
- Representation of the Republic.
- Foreign affairs and national security.
- Presiding over important constitutional processes.
- Appointment powers.
- Use of referendum procedures under constitutional conditions.
- Dissolution powers under constitutional rules.
- Emergency responsibilities.

## 3. The Prime Minister and Government

The Prime Minister:

- Leads the Government.
- Directs the work of ministers.
- Implements national policy.
- Coordinates administration.
- Maintains a relationship with Parliament.
- May be politically accountable to the National Assembly.

The relationship between President and Prime Minister is central to understanding the French semi-presidential model.

## 4. The French Parliament

The French Parliament consists of:

- National Assembly.
- Senate.

### National Assembly

The National Assembly:

- Participates in law-making.
- Debates public policy.
- Exercises oversight.
- May hold the Government politically accountable.
- Represents citizens through elected members.

### Senate

The Senate:

- Represents territorial communities.
- Participates in legislation.
- Reviews bills.
- Provides institutional continuity.
- Performs deliberative and scrutiny functions.

### Comparative Significance

The French model demonstrates:

- Semi-presidential government.
- Dual executive authority.
- Unitary constitutional organization.
- Written and relatively rigid constitutionalism.
- Constitutional review.
- Referendum-based participation.
- Potential cohabitation between President and parliamentary majority.

# Unit IV: Switzerland

## 1. Salient Features of the Constitution

### Written and Lengthy Constitution

Switzerland has a written federal constitutional framework.

It provides for:

- Federal institutions.
- Cantonal authority.
- Individual rights.
- Political participation.
- Distribution of powers.
- Direct-democratic mechanisms.
- Federal and cantonal relations.

### Rigid Constitution

Constitutional amendment involves special procedures and popular participation, making constitutional change more demanding than ordinary legislation.

### Federalism

Switzerland is a federal state consisting of:

- Federal authorities.
- Cantonal governments.
- Municipal institutions.

Federalism supports:

- Territorial autonomy.
- Regional diversity.
- Local participation.
- Division of public functions.
- Institutional cooperation.
- Protection of cantonal authority.

### Direct Democracy

Swiss political life includes direct-democratic instruments such as:

- Referendums.
- Popular initiatives.
- Constitutional voting.
- Citizen participation in public decision-making.

Direct democracy raises questions about:

- Popular sovereignty.
- Majority rule.
- Minority protection.
- Political participation.
- Constitutional amendment.
- Citizen-led policy change.

### Uniqueness of the Political System

The Swiss model is distinctive because it combines:

- Federalism.
- Direct democracy.
- Collegial executive.
- Consensus-oriented politics.
- Multi-party participation.
- Territorial representation.
- Institutional cooperation.

## 2. Plural or Collegial Executive

The Swiss Federal Council functions as a collegial executive.

Characteristics include:

- Collective leadership.
- Shared executive responsibility.
- Multi-party representation.
- Rotation of the presidency.
- Consensus-based decision-making.
- Absence of a single dominant presidential executive.

### Functions

The Federal Council:

- Directs federal administration.
- Implements legislation.
- Develops national policy.
- Represents the federal government.
- Coordinates with cantonal authorities.
- Participates in international affairs.

## 3. Federal Legislature

The Swiss Federal Assembly consists of:

- National Council.
- Council of States.

### National Council

The National Council represents the population.

Its functions include:

- Law-making.
- Budgetary decisions.
- Government scrutiny.
- Representation of citizens.
- Parliamentary debate.

### Council of States

The Council of States represents the cantons.

Its functions include:

- Participation in federal legislation.
- Territorial representation.
- Protection of cantonal interests.
- Review of policy and constitutional matters.

### Comparative Significance

The Swiss model demonstrates:

- Federal government.
- Direct democracy.
- Collegial executive.
- Consensus politics.
- Bicameral representation.
- Strong cantonal participation.
- Constitutional rigidity.
