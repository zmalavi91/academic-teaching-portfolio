## Course Overview

This course introduces students to Political Science as a distinct and interdisciplinary field of study.

It examines the fundamental concepts, ideas, values, institutions, and norms through which political communities are organized and governed. These concepts are essential for understanding the political world and for evaluating how societies design constitutional and policy frameworks to improve human and social life.

The course connects political theory with the study of:

- The state.
- Government.
- Political authority.
- Liberty.
- Equality.
- Justice.
- Rights.
- Democracy.
- Dictatorship.
- Parliamentary government.
- Presidential government.
- Unitary government.
- Federal government.

Students are encouraged to examine both the normative questions of political life and the empirical functioning of political institutions.

The course also develops an introductory understanding of the interdisciplinary nature of Political Science and its relationship with History, Sociology, Economics, Law, International Relations, and related areas of social inquiry.

Political concepts such as liberty, equality, justice, participation, accountability, and the rule of law are also closely connected with contemporary human-rights frameworks. The Universal Declaration of Human Rights, for example, affirms the equal dignity and rights of all human beings and recognizes liberty, equality before the law, and protection against discrimination as central principles. ### 3. Methods of Political Science

#### Normative Method

The normative method examines what political life ought to be.

It asks questions such as:

- What is justice?
- What makes authority legitimate?
- What is the ideal form of government?
- What rights should individuals possess?
- What obligations do citizens and governments have?
- What constitutes a good political society?

#### Empirical Method

The empirical method examines political life as it exists in practice.

It studies:

- Institutions.
- Political behaviour.
- Elections.
- Public opinion.
- Political parties.
- State policies.
- Government performance.
- Social movements.
- Patterns of power and participation.

#### Relationship Between Normative and Empirical Methods

A complete understanding of politics often requires both approaches. Normative analysis evaluates political arrangements according to values and principles, while empirical analysis studies how institutions and political processes function in practice.

## Unit II: The Concept of State

### 1. State: Meaning and Elements

The state is a central concept in Political Science.

The traditional elements of the state include:

- Population.
- Territory.
- Government.
- Sovereignty.

### 2. Nature of the State

The state may be examined as:

- A legal institution.
- A political organization.
- A territorial authority.
- A source of law and public order.
- An instrument of governance.
- A protector of rights.
- An actor in domestic and international politics.

### 3. State and Government

The state and government are related but distinct concepts.

| State | Government |
|---|---|
| Permanent political organization | Institutional machinery that administers the state |
| Includes population and territory | Exercises authority through public institutions |
| Possesses sovereignty | Implements laws and public policies |
| Continues despite changes of government | May change through elections, succession, or political transition |
| Wider and more enduring concept | More specific and operational concept |

### 4. Theories of State Origin

#### Divine Theory

The divine theory explains the origin of the state through divine authority or religious sanction.

Key themes include:

- Divine source of political authority.
- Sacred legitimacy of rulers.
- Obedience as a moral or religious obligation.
- Historical relationship between religion and monarchy.

#### Social Contract Theory

Social contract theories explain political authority through an agreement, actual or hypothetical, among individuals.

Important thinkers include:

- Thomas Hobbes.
- John Locke.
- Jean-Jacques Rousseau.

Major questions include:

- Why do individuals create political authority?
- What obligations arise between citizens and government?
- What happens when government loses legitimacy?
- How are liberty and authority balanced?
